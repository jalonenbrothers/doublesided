## Orientation

This comparison asks whether artificial intelligence will cause mass
technological unemployment, framed at the level of advanced economies
over the next decade. It does not cover: narrow automation of single
industries, AI as a management-surveillance tool, or long-run
(50-year) scenarios. Geography: OECD labour markets, with the UK as
the reference point.

## The case for

Past general-purpose technologies — steam, electricity, computing —
displaced tasks but raised productivity and eventually created more
employment than they destroyed. The IMF estimates AI will affect
almost 40% of global jobs, but "affect" includes complementing work,
not only replacing it [src-imf]. Surveys of employers point in both
directions: the WEF expects 92 million roles displaced but 170 million
created by 2030 — a net gain [src-wef]. Even cautious economists who
expect modest productivity effects do not forecast mass unemployment
in that scenario [src-acemoglu]. The lesson of two centuries of
automation panic is that labour markets absorb shocks when the
technology raises output.

## The case against

AI differs from past waves in one decisive way: it targets cognitive
tasks across nearly every sector simultaneously, removing the escape
route of "move to a safer job". Exposure is highest for white-collar
roles previously considered automation-proof. Even optimists concede
the transition is the danger: retraining pipelines, welfare systems
and regional support are not built for a disruption at this speed
[src-acemoglu]. Optimistic net-job forecasts rely on employer survey
intentions, not observed hiring behaviour [src-wef], and official
bodies themselves warn of rising inequality and a "hollowing out" of
middle-skill work [src-imf]. Opinion reassurance [src-atkinson] is
not evidence.

## Shared ground

Both sides accept that: (1) AI affects a very large share of jobs at
the task level; (2) the net outcome depends on policy, not just
technology; (3) transition costs are real and unevenly distributed;
(4) current statistics cannot yet observe AI-driven displacement
cleanly.

## What the evidence says

Measured macroeconomic effects of AI on aggregate employment remain
close to zero so far — too early for either side's strongest claim.
Task-exposure studies (IMF) are projections, not measurements.
The strongest empirical finding is on inequality within jobs, not job
counts: the gap between high-exposure and low-exposure occupations.

## What remains uncertain

How fast firms actually adopt; whether new roles historically created
by technology (which took decades) can appear in years; the
reliability of employer-intention surveys; and whether productivity
gains materialise at all in the cautious scenario.

## Synthesis

"Take our jobs" is the wrong frame: AI takes tasks, and the evidence
supports task-level disruption with a contested net-employment effect.
The honest answer depends on the speed of the transition and the
policy response — which is where the risk actually sits. Watch
measured adoption rates and retraining outcomes, not forecasts.
